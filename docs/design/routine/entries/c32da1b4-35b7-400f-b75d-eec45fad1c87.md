**CURRENT CANON**

Coherence has deliberately rejected productivity-as-currency. There is no balance, completion reward economy, streak, unlock threshold, mandatory care loop, or penalty for being away. A difficult month must not become visible as environmental decline, and “completion flowers” or similar reward mechanics are explicitly historical/rejected ideas rather than unfinished specifications.

What *is* settled is subtler: the inhabited spaces may gradually reflect **continuity, familiarity, and accumulated context**. Recognition can include starting, returning, reflecting, letting go, and useful departure—not only finishing tasks. The current Home, Today garden, and Library are painted places, but none has an implemented environmental progression system yet.

**DIRECTION**

The environment can become more *yours* over time, but it should not become a meter of how well you are doing.

That distinction matters. If the room gets prettier because you completed twelve tasks, Coherence has recreated a productivity score in decorative form. If the garden wilts because you disappeared for three weeks, absence has become punishment. Both violate the settled philosophy even if no number is shown.

**OPEN QUESTION**

Q6–8 leave the concrete mechanism open: **what should environmental continuity actually change, and what events should cause it, without turning the environment into a hidden performance system?**

I think the most useful move is to stop thinking of this as “growth” in the game-design sense.

**PROPOSAL — Keepsakes, not progress**

The environment should accumulate **small traces of relationship with Coherence**, not upgrades.

Over time, certain meaningful moments could leave behind quiet objects or alterations that say *you have been here before* rather than *you have achieved enough*.

For example, after the user has repeatedly worked with a particular thread, Library might eventually contain a small physical echo of it: a bookmarked volume on the central table, a pressed leaf tucked into a folio, a handwritten label, a sketch pinned near the desk. Not because the thread reached 80% completion, but because it became part of the user’s ongoing life.

Likewise, Home could slowly gain a few familiar personal details: a mug Lumi often uses during body-doubling conversations, a folded blanket appearing after many late-night visits, a small object connected to a recurring reflection. Today’s garden might acquire subtle signs of familiarity—a well-used bench, a particular plant that simply becomes characteristic of the space—not one plant per completed task.

The trigger should therefore be **meaningful recurrence or recognition**, not output.

The underlying rule I’d use is something like:

> The environment may remember what has become familiar, but it does not evaluate whether the user has been good.

That opens up some nice behaviour. Returning after months away does not reveal a neglected, dying room. The room is still there. Perhaps a lamp comes on, Lumi waves, and an old familiar object remains where it was. The message is *continuity survived the gap*.

Letting go could also leave a trace without becoming a trophy. If a long-running intention is consciously released, maybe the related object quietly leaves the working table and eventually appears somewhere more archival in Library. Nothing is awarded. The space simply reflects that the relationship to this thing changed.

I would also avoid deterministic one-to-one mappings like “three Study sessions = plant.” Users will reverse-engineer those almost immediately, and the environment becomes another progress tracker. The system should instead select occasional environmental changes from accumulated context when something has become sufficiently meaningful or familiar. Sparse changes would make each one feel like recognition rather than payout.

Importantly, **environmental continuity should be mostly additive and non-regressive**. Objects may move, be archived, or become less prominent as context changes, but they should not decay because the user stopped showing up. A rough period can make the foreground quieter without making the world visibly worse.

That creates a very different emotional loop from gamification:

**Reward system:**  
“I did enough, so the app gave me something.”

**Coherence continuity:**  
“I’ve lived with this system long enough that the place remembers me.”

That difference feels central to the product.

I would *not* mock this up yet. The visual references establish strong existing rooms, but the important design decision here is the **causal model** behind environmental change. Once “keepsakes rather than progress rewards” is accepted or rejected, individual objects and placements can be designed against the current Home, Today, and Library paintings without accidentally smuggling a reward economy back in.
