## DIRECTION

I think Coherence should stop treating “priority” as something tasks *have* and instead treat it as something the user has **said about a part of their life, for some span of time**.

That sounds small, but it changes the model considerably.

> **Priority should be a scoped claim, not a numeric task property.**

So:

> “Practicum is my main priority this semester.”

is fundamentally different from:

> “The insurance renewal is the thing I should do first today.”

The first describes enduring importance. The second describes present sequencing.

Coherence should be able to hold both without concluding that one of them must be wrong.

The practical design question is:

> **How can Lumi remember what matters without turning the user’s life into a ranked database they have to maintain?**

## PROPOSAL — **Scoped priority claims**

I would give Coherence a lightweight first-class concept underneath the UI: a **priority claim**.

A priority claim is essentially a sentence Lumi can understand and preserve, with a small amount of invisible structure around it:

**Claim:**  
“Practicum is my main priority this semester.”

**Scope:**  
Practicum / School

**Timescale:**  
This semester

**Source:**  
Explicitly stated by the user

**State:**  
Current

There is no `priority = 9`.

There is also no requirement that every Intention belong to one.

The claim exists because it meaningfully changes how Lumi interprets competing demands.

### Different timescales should coexist

Someone might simultaneously have:

> **This semester:** Practicum comes first.

> **This week:** I really need to get my taxes under control.

> **Today:** I want to spend some time on Coherence because I’m excited about it.

Those are not contradictions.

They operate at different scales.

The useful behaviour is that a narrower signal can shape current attention **without rewriting the broader one**.

So Today might legitimately choose:

**Right now:** Make the tax payment  
**After that:** Practicum notes  
**If there’s room:** Coherence design

while Lumi can explain:

> “Practicum is still your main priority this semester. I put the tax payment first because it’s time-sensitive, then protected some practicum time. I kept Coherence in view because you specifically wanted space for it today.”

That is much more truthful than secretly computing three scores and sorting them.

### Urgency should be allowed to outrank sequence without outranking meaning

This is the distinction I think could make Coherence unusually good.

Suppose:

> Practicum is the user’s highest long-term priority.

But:

> Their car insurance expires tomorrow.

Coherence should absolutely be able to recommend dealing with insurance first.

It should **not** update its understanding to:

> Insurance is now more important than practicum.

The selection logic becomes something like:

**What matters?**  
Persistent priority claims.

**What constrains us?**  
Deadlines, commitments, capacity, time available.

**What belongs in the foreground right now?**  
A recommendation produced from both.

That preserves the ontology instead of flattening everything into “priority.”

### New statements should supersede at the appropriate scope

If the user later says:

> “Actually, school isn’t my main priority anymore. I need to stabilize my finances through the end of the year.”

that can supersede the previous semester-scale claim.

The old statement should remain part of history:

> Practicum was previously the main priority.

but not continue influencing recommendations as current truth.

By contrast:

> “Forget practicum today. I desperately need a day off.”

should only change **today’s foreground**.

Tomorrow, the longer-lived practicum priority remains intact.

This gives Coherence a principled answer to something that could otherwise become extremely slippery: **when does a change of mind mean a changed life direction, and when does it mean today is different?**

The language the user used often already contains the answer.

### Ambiguous priority statements can remain soft

Not every statement needs immediate ontology surgery.

> “I really should focus more on my health.”

might be a genuine new priority.

Or it might be frustration spoken once.

Lumi does not need to interrogate it with:

> “Would you like me to save Health as a High Priority?”

Instead, she can treat it as contextual evidence.

If the theme keeps appearing—or begins materially affecting recommendations—she can eventually make the interpretation visible:

> “Health has been coming up a lot lately. I’m starting to treat it as something you want protected alongside school. Does that feel right?”

That is a good point for the claim to become explicit.

This maintains the existing explicit / inferred distinction rather than letting an inference quietly acquire the force of a user instruction.

### Expiry should usually come from meaning, not housekeeping

Temporal language should do most of the work.

> “this week” → naturally expires  
> “until my practicum ends” → conditionally expires  
> “this semester” → bounded period  
> “for now” → deliberately softer, requiring eventual reconsideration

For truly open-ended statements:

> “My family comes first.”

there is no reason to invent an arbitrary 90-day expiration date.

Likewise, I would **not** make users periodically reconfirm priorities.

Coherence should only raise staleness when it matters—for example, if an old claim is materially influencing a recommendation and newer context suggests it may no longer be true.

Then Lumi can say:

> “I’m still treating finishing the certification as a major priority because you told me that earlier this year. Is that still right?”

That is useful maintenance because the question has become consequential.

### This gives “Why this?” a much stronger foundation

Several recent explorations have touched the problem of explaining Today’s selections.

Scoped priority claims solve a large part of that without needing an elaborate explainability interface.

Lumi can simply say:

> “This supports the thing you said matters most this term.”

or, when the recommendation temporarily departs from it:

> “This isn’t your biggest priority overall, but it is the most time-sensitive thing today.”

That distinction is simple enough for the user to understand instantly because it matches ordinary human reasoning.

And if it is wrong:

> “Actually, that isn’t a priority anymore.”

Lumi now knows exactly what kind of correction occurred.

Not merely:

> move task down.

But:

> supersede the underlying claim that was influencing selection.

## A small but important modelling rule

I would make the following precedence explicit:

**An inferred priority may influence a proposal. It may not supersede an explicit priority claim.**

And:

**A narrow explicit priority may temporarily override a broad explicit priority for its own scope without deleting it.**

That produces sensible behaviour without a universal ranking algorithm.

It also makes correction much easier to reason about.

## HISTORICAL / REJECTED

I would avoid:

- numeric priority values such as 1–10;
- High / Medium / Low as the primary model;
- deriving permanent priority from drag order;
- assuming frequently completed work must matter more;
- assuming neglected work matters less;
- one global ordering of every project in someone’s life;
- a weekly “review your priorities” ritual the user must maintain.

Those approaches force the person to maintain a representation of their own life for the software.

Coherence should be doing the opposite.

The canon-level principle I would propose is:

> **Priority is a scoped statement about what matters, not a score attached to work. Narrower attention can temporarily override broader priority without erasing it; urgency can change sequence without changing meaning.**

I’m not generating a mockup for this exploration. The unresolved decision is primarily the **semantic model underneath Today and Lumi’s reasoning**, and the existing interfaces already contain natural places for its effects to appear. Adding a visible priority-management surface now would risk creating exactly the maintenance burden this proposal is intended to avoid.
