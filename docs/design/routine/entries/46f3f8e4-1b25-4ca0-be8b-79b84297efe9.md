## DIRECTION

I think the missing rule is that Lumi’s authority should come from **bounded mandates**, not from an abstract ladder of “safe” versus “unsafe” actions.

The core idea is:

> **Reversibility can reduce friction, but it does not create authority. Authority comes from what the user has actually delegated.**

That matters because a highly reversible action can still be presumptuous. Moving something out of Today is easy to undo, but Lumi should not do it merely because she thinks the user is avoiding it.

Conversely, an action can be meaningful and still need no extra confirmation if the user has already given an exact instruction:

> “Mark the chapter done.”

Asking “Are you sure?” there adds ceremony without protecting agency.

My proposal is:

> **Every consequential action should be traceable to a mandate whose scope actually covers that consequence.**

A mandate is not a permissions screen. It is something the user has communicated naturally.

## PROPOSAL — **Bounded mandates**

When the user says something, Coherence should distinguish between **information**, **delegation**, and **authorization**.

“I have to send Richard the draft by Friday” gives Lumi information about a real commitment. That is enough to update the internal model and quietly note the deadline. It does **not** authorize Lumi to send anything.

“Help me make today manageable” delegates a decision. Lumi now has authority to choose among known intentions and reshape Today within the scope of **today’s attention**. She should not ask approval for each individual reorder.

“Move my dentist appointment to 4:00” is an explicit action instruction. Assuming the target is unambiguous and the capability exists, the instruction itself should count as authorization. Lumi does not need to reply, “Would you like me to move it?”

“Maybe I should move my dentist appointment” is not authorization at all.

The difference is semantic, not based on which screen or tool happens to be involved.

### A mandate should carry scope

The most important property of a mandate is not permission level; it is **scope**.

“Pick what I should work on next” gives Lumi authority to choose the next focus.

It does not give her authority to permanently reprioritize the user’s semester, cancel a commitment, or change tomorrow’s calendar.

“Make this week lighter” can authorize broader temporary pruning of foreground attention.

It still does not authorize deleting underlying intentions.

And:

> “When I tell you I’ve finished something, just mark it done.”

could establish a narrow standing mandate. From then on, “I finished the case notes” can quietly produce **Done** without another question.

The wording of the user’s instruction supplies much of the scope naturally: **this**, **today**, **this session**, **this week**, **from now on**.

That fits nicely with the scoped-priority proposal from earlier runs. Coherence can understand timescale without forcing the user to configure it.

### Internal bookkeeping can follow explicit facts without becoming agency

I would let Lumi quietly perform internal changes that are the straightforward administrative consequence of something the user explicitly said.

If the user says:

> “I finished the ethics paper.”

and Coherence already has an open matching intention, marking it done is bookkeeping. A small **Done** receipt is enough.

If they say:

> “Actually, the paper isn’t due until next Thursday.”

correcting the due date is bookkeeping.

If they say:

> “That email has nothing to do with my practicum.”

removing an inferred Thread association is bookkeeping.

These actions are not Lumi deciding what the user should do. They are keeping the model coherent with what the user just told her.

I would not repeatedly ask:

> “Would you like me to update that?”

That makes the user maintain the system through confirmation dialogue.

### Delegated decisions should be executable, not endlessly proposed

This is where many assistants become frustrating.

The user says:

> “I’m overwhelmed. Just choose something reasonable for me to start.”

Lumi selects an item and then responds:

> “Would you like me to make that your Right now?”

That forces the user to perform the very decision they delegated.

Under bounded mandates, **Help me choose** or an equivalent conversational request grants Lumi temporary authority to actually establish the recommendation as the current foreground.

The receipt can say:

> **Reshaped Today**

and the conversational explanation can remain light.

If Lumi misunderstands, **Not this** or ordinary conversation corrects it.

This is exactly where reversibility is useful: because the action is easy to undo and is directly inside the delegated scope, Coherence can remove confirmation friction.

### External actions need authorization, not necessarily an extra confirmation dialog

I would avoid the simplistic rule:

> External action = always ask twice.

The important question is whether the user has already clearly authorized **that exact consequence**.

Compare:

> “Richard needs the draft.”

No sending authority.

> “I should probably email Richard tonight.”

Still no sending authority.

> “Draft an email to Richard.”

Authority to compose, not send.

> “Send that to Richard.”

Authority to send.

That progression feels completely ordinary in conversation. Coherence should respect it rather than adding a separate permission grammar.

The same principle applies to calendar actions:

> “I wish I had more time before that meeting.”

is context.

> “Could you find another time?”

delegates search/proposal.

> “Move it to Wednesday at 2.”

authorizes the change.

Uncertainty can still force clarification. If there are two Richards or three Wednesday appointments, Lumi does not have a sufficiently precise mandate yet.

### Durable authority should never be inferred from tolerance

This is an important boundary.

Suppose Lumi proposes reshaping Today five times and the user accepts every time.

That does **not** establish:

> “Lumi may now reshape Today whenever she wants.”

Repeated acceptance is evidence that the proposals may be useful. It is not permission.

Likewise, repeated correction does not automatically establish a prohibition unless the user actually expresses one.

A durable mandate should come from explicit language:

> “You don’t have to ask me about that anymore.”

> “Always check with me before moving appointments.”

> “Don’t change Today unless I ask.”

That keeps authorization aligned with the canon’s explicit-versus-inferred distinction.

### Receipts become more valuable than confirmations

The existing quiet ledger lines are a particularly good fit here.

A low-friction interaction becomes:

> “I finished the reading, but I’m too tired for the paper.”

**Done — Read Chapter 4**  
**Reshaped Today**

Then Lumi:

> “Okay. I’ve kept the paper out of the foreground for tonight.”

The user can see what changed without having had to approve each database mutation.

That is a much stronger division of labour:

**user supplies meaning and agency → Lumi supplies administration → interface supplies visibility.**

A receipt can also carry an unobtrusive **undo/correct** affordance where appropriate, but it should not become a toast storm.

### This also clarifies the boundary with Q9

I would keep **Q9 — What may Lumi initiate?** separate.

A mandate answers:

> **If we are already here, what am I authorized to do?**

Q9 answers:

> **When am I allowed to interrupt or initiate something in the first place?**

A standing mandate such as “you can automatically mark things done when I tell you I finished them” does not mean Lumi may start sending unsolicited reminders.

That separation prevents autonomy from quietly turning into proactivity.

## HISTORICAL / REJECTED

I would reject two opposite patterns.

The first is **confirmation theatre**: Lumi asks “Would you like me to…?” after every statement, including when the user already gave a clear instruction. That preserves technical control while increasing executive burden.

The second is **helpful overreach**: because an action is reversible, Lumi decides she may perform it whenever her model thinks it would be useful. Reversibility is not consent.

I would also avoid a large **Lumi Permissions** matrix in Settings with toggles such as “May reorder tasks / may update deadlines / may infer priorities / may edit calendar.” That asks the user to predict every future context and administer the assistant before the assistant can reduce administration.

The canon-level principle I would propose is:

> **Lumi acts within bounded mandates. Explicit facts authorize faithful bookkeeping; delegated decisions authorize action within their stated scope; consequential actions require authorization that actually covers the consequence. Reversibility lowers confirmation friction but never creates authority by itself.**
