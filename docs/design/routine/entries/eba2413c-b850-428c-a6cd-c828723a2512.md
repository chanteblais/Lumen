### CURRENT CANON

I grounded this run in **COHERENCE_START_HERE**, **COHERENCE_CURRENT_STATE**, **COHERENCE_DESIGN_CANON**, **COHERENCE_OPEN_QUESTIONS**, and **COHERENCE_RECENT_CHANGES**.

A core settled rule is already doing a lot of work here: **automate administration, never agency**. Explicit user instructions retain authority within their scope, Lumi should propose rather than quietly take over, and a guess must not overwrite something the user has actually said. At the same time, unnecessary confirmation is also a burden. If the user says “mark that done,” asking “Are you sure?” is not respectful agency; it is friction.

The implementation already reflects part of this distinction. Lumi can create, update, complete, reopen, and drop internal intentions conversationally, while broader external actions such as rescheduling appointments, deleting consequential information, or acting on mail are not established as general autonomous behaviour.

### DIRECTION

Coherence should eventually feel as though Lumi can **take care of things for you** without creating the anxiety of wondering what she might do behind your back.

That means “autonomy” should not be one global permission like *Lumi may act / Lumi may not act*. The useful unit is the **scope of a particular delegation**.

### OPEN QUESTION

**Q10 — What can Lumi do autonomously?**

The existing question already points toward the right dimensions: uncertainty, consequence, and reversibility. I think it needs one additional dimension:

**social externality**.

Some actions are technically reversible but still cross a meaningful boundary. Sending an email may have an “undo send” window, but another human can read it. Cancelling an appointment can sometimes be rebooked, but it affects another person. Moving a task from Tuesday to Thursday is very different.

So the real design problem is:

> **When does Lumi have enough authority to cross from interpreting the user’s intention into committing the user to a changed world?**

### PROPOSAL — **Delegation envelopes**

Rather than assigning Lumi a fixed autonomy level, Coherence should infer or receive a **delegation envelope** around each instruction: what Lumi is allowed to change, for how long, and how far she may go without checking again.

The principle would be:

> **Lumi can act without confirmation when the user’s intent is clear, the action stays inside the understood scope, and reversing it would not create meaningful cost or consequence.**

That creates four useful behavioural bands.

**Silent context maintenance** should require no confirmation at all. Lumi can reconcile duplicated context, notice that “Richard” in two conversations refers to the same ongoing Thread, retain that an appointment occurred, or update relevance behind the scenes. Inferences remain identifiable as inferences and cannot overwrite explicit user claims, but maintaining the shared model should not produce constant permission prompts.

**Reversible internal actions** can usually happen immediately when the instruction is clear. If the user says “That’s done,” Lumi marks it done. If they say “Put the insurance stuff under Personal,” she can refile it. If they say “Actually, not today,” Today can reshape. The interface should show the existing quiet ledger-style receipt and offer undo where useful, rather than interrupting beforehand.

The important distinction is that **Lumi does not need confirmation for an instruction the user just explicitly gave her**. Confirmation belongs around ambiguity or consequence, not around every action.

**Consequential internal changes** deserve a preview when the user has not quite committed yet. A useful example is:

> “I think I need to push the paper to next week.”

That is not necessarily the same as:

> “Move the paper to next week.”

In the first case Lumi might answer:

> “That would free up this week, but it puts the first draft only three days before your deadline. I’d keep one small writing block this week and move the rest.”

The user is still deciding. Lumi should not translate reflective language into a calendar change just because she can.

Once the user says:

> “Yes, do that.”

the envelope becomes clear and Lumi acts.

**External or socially consequential actions** should have the highest threshold. Sending messages, cancelling or booking appointments, purchasing something, deleting material that cannot be easily restored, or changing something another person relies on should normally receive a concrete preview before commitment.

Not a generic modal saying **Are you sure?**

Something more like:

> **I’ll move Tuesday’s supervision to the 7 p.m. opening and decline the existing 6 p.m. invitation.**

> **Confirm**

The preview should tell the user exactly what will enter the world.

### Explicit delegation can widen the envelope

This is where the system could become genuinely useful rather than perpetually cautious.

The user might tell Lumi:

> “When Richard sends me possible supervision times, add the ones that fit my availability to my calendar. You don’t need to ask me every time.”

That should create a **narrow standing delegation**, not a global calendar permission.

Lumi now has authority to:

- act on messages from Richard,
- concerning supervision,
- using already-known availability,
- by adding suitable options to the calendar.

She does **not** gain permission to reschedule therapy appointments, respond to Richard on the user’s behalf, or reorganize the rest of the week.

If the situation falls outside that envelope—perhaps all offered times conflict—Lumi returns to proposal:

> “None of Richard’s options fit the availability you gave me. Want to look at what could move?”

That feels much more Coherence-like than a permissions matrix.

### The envelope should also expire naturally

Delegation needs timescale.

> “Handle this for me today”

is different from:

> “You can always do this.”

And even an open-ended delegation should be revisable conversationally:

> “Actually, ask me before changing my calendar from now on.”

That instruction should immediately narrow Lumi’s authority without requiring the user to hunt through settings.

A future Settings surface could expose standing delegations for inspection, but ordinary use should not require maintaining them there. Conversation remains the easiest way to grant or revoke authority.

### Why this matters for Lumi’s personality

This would let her become more capable **without becoming more controlling**.

A weak assistant asks for permission constantly.

An unsafe assistant decides too much.

A Coherence-shaped assistant knows the difference between:

> “I understand what you want.”

and:

> “I have authority to make that true.”

That distinction also pairs neatly with the earlier proposal about proactivity. **Proactivity answers when Lumi may speak. Delegation envelopes answer when Lumi may act.**

Together they give her substantially more initiative without eroding user authority.

### HISTORICAL / REJECTED BOUNDARY

I would avoid a global **Autonomy: Low / Medium / High** setting. It collapses radically different domains into one preference and forces users to anticipate every future consequence.

I would also reject confirmation-before-every-tool-call. That sounds safe but would make Coherence exhausting: “Mark it done?” “Move this?” “Remember that?” “Update Today?” The user ends up administrating the assistant.

The canon-level principle I would add is:

> **Authority is scoped, not global. Lumi acts freely inside a clear delegation envelope and returns to the user at its boundary.**

I would not generate a mockup for this exploration. The meaningful decision is the authority model beneath many existing surfaces; introducing a new permissions UI before that model is settled would put the interface ahead of the product logic.
