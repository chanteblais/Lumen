## Purpose

This document defines the spatial architecture of Coherence: the idea that major product functions are experienced as places with distinct cognitive and emotional roles, rather than as a collection of unrelated productivity modules.

The current core spaces are:

Home — arrive, inhabit, converse\
Today / Garden — tend\
Lists / Library — organize, retrieve, orient\
Focus / Study — attend

These correspond to familiar functions—conversation, daily planning, broader organization, and focus—but the environmental model gives each a deeper experiential purpose.

The environments are not visual themes applied to conventional screens.

Each space should embody the cognitive activity that happens there.

## 1. Why Coherence has spaces

Most productivity software is organized around functions: Dashboard, Tasks, Calendar, Projects, Analytics, Focus.

That architecture is efficient, but it tends to frame productivity as operating machinery.

Coherence is interested in a different experience.

A user may arrive uncertain, overwhelmed, ready to work, reflective, scattered, curious, returning after an absence, trying to remember something, trying to decide what matters, or already knowing exactly what needs doing.

These states require different relationships to information and attention.

The spaces of Coherence provide contexts for those relationships.

The user does not merely select a feature. They move somewhere appropriate to what they are trying to do.

## 2. Coherence has a geography

Home\
Cognitive mode: arriving, reflecting, talking, orienting.\
Relationship: inhabiting.\
Question: What is happening? What do I need?

Garden\
Cognitive mode: prioritizing, choosing, tending, adapting to capacity.\
Relationship: cultivation.\
Question: What deserves attention today?

Library\
Cognitive mode: organizing, retrieving, surveying, rearranging.\
Relationship: stewardship of held context.\
Question: What am I carrying, and where does it belong?

Study\
Cognitive mode: initiation, sustained attention, body doubling, re-entry.\
Relationship: attending.\
Question: What are we doing now?

These are not four independent tools. They are four ways of interacting with the same underlying life context. Lumi moves between them with the user.

## 3. Navigation should remain understandable

*Current label decision: Library remains the user-facing name already chosen. Functional labels below illustrate clarity, not a requirement to rename it Lists. Since 2026-09-13 the nav also carries **Lists**, a sheet over the page you're on with everything on your lists — a view, not a space ([decision](../living/decisions.md#2026-09-13--lists-is-in-the-nav-again-as-a-sheet-over-the-page-youre-on)). Other navigation choices remain open.*

The metaphorical architecture should deepen the experience without obscuring basic usability.

The user should not need to learn that an obscure poetic label means daily planning.

Primary navigation can remain straightforward:

Home\
Today\
Lists\
Focus

while the environmental identity becomes apparent through the experience itself.

Internally and in design documentation, we may refer to Home, Garden, Library, and Study.

The precise user-facing labels can evolve through testing.

Poetry should deepen clarity, not replace it.

## 4. Spaces represent cognitive modes, not feature ownership

A feature should not automatically receive a new space.

The question is not: Where do we put this feature?

The better question is: What cognitive mode does this interaction belong to?

The same underlying Action or Intention may appear in several spaces.

In the Library, the user may inspect and reorganize it.\
In the Garden, it may be surfaced because it deserves attention today.\
In the Study, it becomes the object of focused work.\
At Home, the user may simply talk to Lumi about why they have been avoiding it.

The object remains the same. What changes is the relationship to it.

This distinction should prevent the product from fragmenting into modules and duplicate state.

## 5. The spaces are projections over shared state

Home, Garden, Library, and Study should not maintain separate versions of the user's life.

They are projections over one evolving model.

Conceptually:

life\
→ evolving model\
→ relevant context\
→ space-specific projection

A conversation at Home can alter what appears in the Library. A manual priority change in the Library can alter the Garden. A Garden selection can become the Study's current object of attention. What happens in the Study updates the shared model and is already known when the user returns Home.

Context travels.

## 6. Home — arriving and inhabiting

Home is the primary soft landing of Coherence.

It should feel safe, familiar, contemplative, quiet, nostalgic, inhabited, and low-demand.

Home is not a dashboard.

It should not confront the user with everything requiring attention.

Its fundamental message is:

You can arrive here before you know what you're doing.

Lumi is present.

The user can talk, think aloud, brain dump, ask what matters today, return after an absence, reflect, complain, explore an idea, ask for help, or begin without a structured request.

Home is where the relationship with Lumi is most conversational.

It is also where the distinction between thought and task matters most. Not everything said at Home should be converted into productivity structure. Sometimes the user is simply thinking.

## 7. Home should become familiar

The word Home should eventually feel meaningful.

The user returns to the same place. Lumi inhabits it. There may be subtle continuity across time.

The environment might slowly acquire traces of history or preferences, but Home should not become a customizable virtual room requiring maintenance.

The user should not have to decorate it, purchase furniture, organize objects, unlock items, or maintain the environment.

Its familiarity should emerge without creating another system to manage.

The ideal feeling is: I've been here before.

Not: I need to configure my Home screen.

## 8. Today — the Garden

Today is represented conceptually as a Garden.

Traditional task systems ask: What remains incomplete?

The Garden asks: What needs tending today?

A garden naturally contains things in different states. Some things are newly planted, actively growing, waiting, resting, needing attention, ready to finish, or no longer worth tending.

Most importantly:

A gardener does not tend every plant every day.

This makes the Garden particularly appropriate for capacity-aware daily planning.

## 9. The Garden is an attention environment

The Garden is not the user's task database.

It is a temporary, contextual interpretation of what deserves cognitive foreground today.

The broader model may contain dozens of unresolved things. The Garden should often show very few.

Its deepest function is compression and containment:

There are many things in your life. Only a few need your attention here today.

The user should increasingly trust that what is absent from the Garden has not been forgotten.

## 10. The Garden metaphor should remain partly implicit

The Garden should not become a gamified task garden.

Avoid simplistic mappings such as every task is a flower, completing a task makes it bloom, or Focus minutes water plants.

Instead, gardening should influence the relationship and vocabulary of the space.

Useful conceptual terms may include:

Plant — begin an intention.\
Tend — continue something underway.\
Let rest — intentionally leave something alone.\
Prune — remove something that no longer matters.\
Harvest — bring something meaningfully to completion.

These terms should only appear in the interface when they feel natural and immediately understandable.

The metaphor should deepen the experience, not create another system to learn.

## 11. A well-tended Garden represents continuity

The Garden may visually develop over time.

A new user might begin with open soil, young plants, simple paths, and substantial breathing room.

A long-used Garden might feel established, layered, cared for, alive, and gently abundant.

This development should not correspond directly to productivity output.

A lush Garden should not mean: This user completes lots of tasks.

It should mean something closer to: This is a place that has been inhabited and tended over time.

Growth represents continuity, relationship, and accumulated history—not achievement.

## 12. Lists — the Library

Lists is conceptually represented as a Library.

The Library is where more of the broader structure Lumi is holding becomes visible and manipulable.

Initial categories may include School, Work, Personal, Ideas, and Later, but the long-term Library can become richer than static lists.

The core metaphor is:

Things have places, but they remain retrievable and rearrangeable.

The Library represents organization without demanding constant administration.

## 13. The Library is not merely storage

The Library represents the user's accumulated threads.

Over time it may hold intentions, projects, tasks, ideas, recurring responsibilities, useful context, histories of work, relationships between things, and perhaps knowledge or notes.

Its long-term value may be partly archival.

The Library can answer:

What am I carrying?

but eventually also:

What have I been thinking about?\
Where did this thread begin?\
What became of that idea?\
What belongs together?

Its complexity should emerge gradually rather than being imposed at onboarding.

## 14. The Library grows through expansion, not points

As the user's accumulated context grows, the Library may naturally become more extensive.

New shelving, areas, or sections may emerge around actual recurring domains of the user's life.

For one user these might become School, Psychology, Personal, Writing.\
For another: Work, Family, Music, House.

The user is not unlocking Library sections.

The Library is making room.

Expansion reflects accumulated life, not earned progress.

## 15. The Library should remain manipulable

Although Lumi does much of the organizational work, the Library is where manual interaction can be especially valuable.

The user may reorder things, move them between contexts, inspect them, correct Lumi's assumptions, change priority, put something aside, or retrieve something forgotten.

Dragging can carry semantic meaning.

Vertical movement may express relative priority.\
Movement between sections may express category or context.\
Movement to Later may mean: stop foregrounding this for now.

These actions should update Lumi's understanding.

The Library is a visible, manipulable representation of shared understanding—not a separate database from the AI.

## 16. The Library should remain navigable as it grows

A real library can contain thousands of books because it has systems for finding them.

As Coherence accumulates context, it should become more useful, not increasingly overwhelming.

Potential mechanisms include natural-language retrieval through Lumi, semantic grouping, emergent sections, zooming into shelves or areas, contextual search, active versus archival layers, and visual indications of related Threads.

The user should never be expected to manually maintain an enormous taxonomy.

Lumi should help the Library organize itself.

## 17. Focus — the Study

Focus is represented conceptually as the Study.

The Study exists for one purpose:

Give attention to one thing.

When the user enters, most of Coherence should recede.

The Study may contain the current Action or Intention, an immediate first action, optional timer, minimal controls, and Lumi.

Very little else.

The environmental transition itself can communicate:

We came here to do this now.

## 18. The Study embodies body doubling

The Study is where Lumi's presence may become especially meaningful.

The user can say: Stay with me while I work on this.

Lumi may settle somewhere nearby. She might sit, read, quietly occupy herself, breathe, blink, or occasionally look up.

She should not continually speak.

The Study should feel like shared quiet attention.

If the user becomes stuck, Lumi becomes available.\
If they become distracted, Lumi helps them return.\
If momentum takes over, Lumi gets out of the way.

## 19. Leaving the Study is not failure

Focus sessions should not create a performance contract.

If the user stops early, gets distracted, changes tasks, needs a break, or abandons the session, the Study should not mark the attempt as failed.

Lumi may simply help determine: Where are we now?

The Study exists to support attention, not measure obedience to a timer.

## 20. Lumi is the continuity between spaces

The environments would risk feeling like separate themed tools without Lumi.

Lumi connects them.

At Home: We talk.\
In the Garden: We decide what deserves tending.\
In the Library: We organize what we're carrying.\
In the Study: We work together.

Lumi carries context between them.

This gives the user the sense that they are moving through one coherent environment with the same companion rather than launching separate productivity modules.

## 21. Information should travel with the user

If something is discussed at Home, it may become structured context in the Library.

If the user changes its priority in the Library, that should affect what appears in the Garden.

If the Garden identifies it as the current foreground, the user can enter the Study with it.

If work happens in the Study, Lumi should know that when the user returns Home.

Conceptually:

Home — conversation / meaning\
↓\
Library — structure / held context\
↓\
Garden — selection / today's attention\
↓\
Study — present attention / action\
↓\
shared state updates everywhere

This is not necessarily a linear workflow. The user may move freely between spaces.

## 22. Movement between spaces should feel continuous

Navigation should be fast and practical.

Environmental design should not introduce unnecessary loading, cinematic transitions, or interaction friction.

But subtle transitions may reinforce spatial continuity.

Lumi may walk toward another area. Materials or lighting may shift. Architectural motifs may carry between spaces. Transitions may imply movement without literally animating a journey.

Atmosphere should accompany navigation, not obstruct it.

## 23. The spaces should feel related

Home, Garden, Library, and Study should belong to the same world.

They should share material language, typography, architectural sensibility, colour relationships, age and history, environmental logic, and Lumi.

Each space can have a distinct emotional register while remaining recognizably part of Coherence.

The overall visual language currently draws from:

antique book × quiet inhabited world × crisp modern interface

The environmental layer adds character. The interface layer preserves clarity.

## 24. Environments should not become scenery for scenery's sake

The spaces can be beautiful, but decorative richness should be treated carefully.

We have explicitly rejected interface imagery that feels like generic lifestyle decoration: arbitrary coffee cups, decorative book stacks, wellness imagery, and stock coziness.

Environmental elements should contribute to place, meaning, continuity, interaction, or atmosphere.

A Library can contain books because it is a Library. That does not mean every UI card needs decorative books.

A Garden can contain plants because cultivation is the governing metaphor. That does not mean every task needs botanical decoration.

The environment earns imagery that the interface itself does not.

## 25. Environmental richness and interface quiet are separate dimensions

A space may eventually be visually rich while the functional interface remains extremely simple.

A mature Garden may contain established paths, trees, flowers, walls, and distant landscape while the productivity information visible is only:

Right now — Finish discussion post.\
After that — Send invoice.

This is desirable.

Richness of environment does not imply density of obligation.

Likewise, a sparse new Garden should not imply that the user is failing. It simply represents a space earlier in its history.

## 26. Spaces can change with the relationship

The environments may gradually respond to accumulated use.

Potential development includes subtle environmental growth, new areas becoming relevant, signs of previous activity, objects associated with recurring contexts, archival expansion, and Lumi developing additional behaviours within familiar places.

These changes should feel discovered rather than awarded.

Ideally the user occasionally notices: Oh. That's new.

Not: Congratulations! You unlocked Garden Level 4\.

## 27. Avoid productivity gamification

The environmental architecture creates obvious opportunities for gamification. Most should be resisted.

Avoid XP, levels, task-completion currencies, daily streak rewards, unlocking environments through productivity, achievement badges, completion-driven decoration, and punishing environmental decay.

The Garden should not die because the user disappeared.\
The Library should not become dusty because they missed a week.\
Lumi should not become sad because they failed to complete tasks.

The world should welcome re-entry.

## 28. Growth should not become judgment

If environments change over time, users may infer meaning from those changes.

Avoid beautiful environment = productive person and sparse environment = failing person.

Environmental development should primarily reflect duration, accumulated context, exploration, relationship, diversity of activity, and inhabitation rather than raw output.

A person living through a difficult period should not watch their world deteriorate as punishment.

## 29. Spaces should support different levels of engagement

Not every user will want to engage deeply with the environmental layer.

Someone should be able to use Coherence pragmatically: open Home, ask Lumi, start a task, leave.

Another user may enjoy wandering into the Library, watching the Garden develop, noticing Lumi's behaviours, and exploring accumulated history.

Both are valid.

The world should reward attention with meaning, not require attention for functionality.

## 30. Lumi may eventually move independently

Long-term, Lumi can become more spatially present.

She might walk around Home, wander through the Garden, move between Library shelves, settle into the Study, inspect objects, climb ladders, or quietly occupy herself.

Her movement should make the world feel inhabited.

It should not turn Coherence into a virtual-pet system.

Lumi does not need care from the user. She provides companionship and continuity.

## 31. Spatial presence should not imply surveillance

Lumi inhabiting a room does not mean she should appear to watch the user constantly.

Especially in the Study, she may orient toward her own quiet activity.

Presence should feel companionable rather than monitoring.

The user should feel: Lumi is here.

Not: Lumi is watching whether I work.

## 32. Spaces can support progressive disclosure

The spatial metaphor provides a natural way to reveal complexity gradually.

The user may first see a room or broad area, then move closer to a shelf, Thread, plan, or object of attention.

This is especially promising in the Library, where breadth can exist without being presented all at once.

Spatial depth should support cognitive depth.

It should not become a maze.

## 33. Spatial metaphor should not determine the data model

The Library may look like shelves. The Garden may look cultivated. The Study may look like a quiet room.

That does not mean the underlying information architecture should be forced into literal spatial objects.

Threads do not need database coordinates because they appear on a shelf.

Environmental representation is a projection of the model, not the model itself.

This preserves flexibility across desktop, mobile, accessibility modes, and future interfaces.

## 34. Spaces should preserve orientation

The user should generally know:

where they are,\
what kind of thinking belongs here,\
and how to move somewhere else.

Environmental richness should not obscure navigation.

Stable navigation, recurring visual grammar, and Lumi's continuity can all help.

The product may feel like a world without requiring the user to navigate it like a game world.

## 35. Mobile should preserve cognitive spaces rather than literal rooms

On a small screen, it may not be practical to render the same environmental compositions used on desktop.

The priority is preserving each space's cognitive relationship:

Home — arrival and conversation.\
Garden — today's foreground.\
Library — broader held context.\
Study — one thing.

The environmental metaphor can become more suggestive on mobile while the information hierarchy remains intact.

## 36. Accessibility should survive the metaphor

No essential meaning should depend solely on spatial location, animation, drag gestures, environmental imagery, or colour.

The spaces should remain understandable through semantic structure, text, keyboard navigation, non-drag alternatives, sufficient contrast, and reduced-motion modes.

The environmental layer enriches usability. It must not become a prerequisite for usability.

## 37. The world should not demand maintenance

This principle applies across every space.

The user should not have to:

water the Garden,\
clean the Study,\
organize every Library shelf,\
decorate Home,\
or care for Lumi

in order for Coherence to function well.

Any maintenance required by the world would recreate the very executive burden the product is designed to reduce.

The world should increasingly maintain itself.

## 38. The spaces externalize different forms of cognitive burden

Each space helps Coherence carry something the user would otherwise need to hold mentally.

Home carries ambiguity.

The user can arrive before knowing what they need.

The Library carries breadth.

The user does not need to remember everything at once.

The Garden carries prioritization.

The user does not need to compare every obligation every day.

The Study carries containment.

The user does not need to hold everything while attending to one thing.

This gives the spatial architecture a functional purpose beyond aesthetics.

## 39. The spaces perform progressive compression

The spaces can be understood as different degrees of compression over the user's active-life model.

Library — broader held context.

Garden — what deserves attention today.

Study — what deserves attention now.

Home is different: it provides conversational access to the model without requiring the user to see its structure.

Conceptually:

life\
→ evolving model\
→ relevant context\
→ Today\
→ now

The spaces make this compression experiential.

## 40. The world should support re-entry

Returning to Coherence should feel like returning somewhere familiar.

The environment can help provide continuity even when the user's life has become fragmented.

Home remains recognizable.\
The Garden remains welcoming.\
The Library still holds what was left there.\
The Study can help reconstruct where work stopped.\
Lumi remains the familiar companion across all of them.

The world should never punish absence.

## 41. Familiarity should matter more than novelty

Environmental design should resist constant visual novelty.

The emotional value of a place comes partly from recognizing it.

Changes should generally be slow enough that the user occasionally notices them rather than continually being presented with new scenery.

The desired relationship is closer to inhabiting a familiar place than consuming an endless stream of new environments.

## 42. Environmental memory can become meaningful

Over time, spaces may preserve subtle traces of history.

A path may feel established.\
A Library area may have grown.\
Home may contain a familiar object.\
The Garden may feel older.

These traces should not map mechanically to completed Actions.

Their purpose is to communicate:

Time has passed here. This place has been lived in.

This may eventually become an important part of Coherence's sense of continuity and nostalgia.

## 43. The spaces should not totalize the user's life

Coherence may develop a rich internal world, but it should never imply that the user's actual life exists inside the application.

The spaces are interfaces for attention and context.

They are not replacements for lived experience.

A successful Garden often helps the user leave and do something elsewhere.\
A successful Study may disappear once momentum takes over.\
Home does not need to mediate every reflection.\
The Library does not need to contain every thought the user has ever had.

The world should support life outside itself.

## 44. New spaces require justification

Future features should not automatically produce new rooms.

Before creating another space, ask:

Does this represent a genuinely different cognitive relationship?

Can the interaction belong naturally to Home, Garden, Library, or Study?

Would another space clarify the product, or fragment it?

Does the user need to understand this as a distinct place?

The current four-space model should be treated as intentionally compact.

## 45. Spaces may contain subspaces without becoming primary navigation

As the product grows, environments may contain smaller areas.

The Library may have archives or reading areas.\
Home may have places Lumi tends to occupy.\
The Garden may contain distinct regions.\
The Study may have different working configurations.

These do not necessarily need to become primary navigation destinations.

Spatial richness can grow within a stable high-level architecture.

## 46. Environmental metaphors have boundaries

The metaphors should be used where they clarify the relationship.

Home is strong for arrival, familiarity, and conversation.\
Garden is strong for cultivation, selective care, growth, and rest.\
Library is strong for organization, retrieval, breadth, memory, and accumulated context.\
Study is strong for attention, quiet work, and body doubling.

Do not force unrelated concepts into those metaphors simply to preserve thematic consistency.

Plain interface language is often preferable to strained metaphor.

## 47. The user should not need to understand the architecture intellectually

These documents articulate the spatial model explicitly because designers and implementers need to understand it.

The user does not.

They should experience:

Home feels like somewhere I can arrive.\
Today makes the day feel manageable.\
Lists help me find what I'm carrying.\
Focus helps me stay with one thing.\
Lumi seems to know how these pieces relate.

If the user must read an explanation of the four cognitive modes before the product makes sense, the design has failed.

## 48. Spatial design should follow product behaviour

The spaces are not a branding layer added after the product is built.

Their visual and interaction design should emerge from their cognitive role.

The Garden is selective because Today is selective.\
The Study is sparse because attention has narrowed.\
The Library can reveal breadth because its purpose is retrieval and organization.\
Home can remain open because the user may not yet know what they need.

Form follows cognitive function.

## 49. The four spaces in one sentence each

Home:\
A familiar place where the user can arrive, think, and talk to Lumi before needing structure.

Garden:\
A cultivated attention environment where Lumi compresses the broader life model into what deserves tending today.

Library:\
A navigable representation of broader held context where the user and Lumi can organize, retrieve, and correct shared understanding.

Study:\
A quiet attention environment where the world narrows to one thing and Lumi becomes a body-double presence.

## 50. Core spatial design test

When reviewing a space, ask:

Does this environment embody a cognitive relationship rather than merely decorate a feature?

Is it immediately understandable what kind of activity belongs here?

Does it operate on shared state rather than creating a separate silo?

Does Lumi carry continuity into and out of it?

Does the environment reduce cognitive burden rather than add interaction burden?

Can the interface remain quiet even when the environment becomes rich?

Can the user ignore the environmental layer and still use the product effectively?

Does the space welcome re-entry after absence?

Does environmental growth represent continuity rather than achievement?

Are we adding imagery because it belongs to the place, or because the screen feels empty?

Would this interaction still make sense if the environmental metaphor were removed?

And most importantly:

Does this space help Coherence hold something the user would otherwise have to hold themselves?

If not, reconsider it.

## 51. Spatial north star

Coherence should feel less like operating productivity software and more like moving between a small number of familiar places that support different relationships to attention.

Home lets the user arrive.

The Library holds the breadth of what matters.

The Garden narrows that breadth to what deserves tending today.

The Study narrows it again to what deserves attention now.

Lumi moves through all of them with the same understanding.

The spaces are therefore not merely a visual identity for Coherence.

They are an experiential expression of the product's deepest promise:

Coherence can hold more so the user can hold less.
